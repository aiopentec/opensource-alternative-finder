Proprietary video conferencing tools often introduce hidden costs around data privacy, meeting time limits, and licensing creep. Engineering teams, privacy-conscious organizations, and IT administrators frequently look toward open-source alternatives to regain absolute control over their communication infrastructure. When evaluating these platforms, several critical factors demand consideration. First, self-hosting effort varies wildly; some solutions deploy with a simple docker compose file, while others require complex scaling architectures involving selective forwarding units like Jicofo and Jitsi Videobridge. Data ownership is another primary driver, ensuring that telemetry, chat logs, and media streams never transit third-party servers. Feature parity matters for user adoption—virtual backgrounds, screen sharing, and recording capabilities must meet daily workflow demands without friction. Finally, team size and bandwidth infrastructure dictate whether you can reliably host on-premise or need a hybrid cloud topology. This page covers direct, practical comparisons to help you navigate these architectural and operational trade-offs, starting with our evaluation of Jitsi Meet as a drop-in replacement for Zoom.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can Jitsi Meet completely replace Zoom for large enterprise webinars?

Jitsi Meet excels at ad-hoc and team meetings, but scaling it beyond 100 participants requires tuning server resources and implementing a selective forwarding unit cluster. For standard team collaboration, it matches Zoom's core video and screen sharing capabilities without requiring client-side software installation.

How difficult is it to self-host an open-source video conferencing tool?

Deployment complexity depends entirely on the platform and your expected concurrency. Solutions like Jitsi can be spun up on a single Linux VPS in under an hour using Docker, but maintaining low-latency audio and video streams at scale requires understanding TURN/STUN server configuration and network bandwidth provisioning.

Do open-source video conferencing alternatives support end-to-end encryption?

Most major open-source meeting platforms support encryption in transit using TLS and DTLS, and several implement end-to-end encryption for browser-based calls using modern standards like the WebRTC Insertable Streams API. However, enabling E2EE often disables certain server-side features like cloud recording and dial-in gateways.
